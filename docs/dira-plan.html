DIRA

Website Build Plan Draft 02 24 Aug 2026

A clinic website that reads like it was written by a doctor, not generated.

The source copy for DIRA is already strong — specific, restrained, and honest about uncertainty. The risk isn't the writing. It's the packaging. This plan sets out what makes a site look machine-made, how we avoid each tell, and the order we build in.

Section 01The brief

What we're building

A nine-page marketing and patient-information site for DIRA — Deshpande Immunology & Rheumatology Association — a single-consultant rheumatology and clinical immunology practice led by Dr Gaurang Deshpande.

The site has three jobs, in this order:

  1. Convert the uncertain patient. Most visitors won't have a diagnosis. They'll have joint pain, a positive ANA, or a report they don't understand. The site must tell them they're in the right place before it tells them anything else.
  2. Establish credentials fast. MD Internal Medicine, DM Clinical Immunology & Rheumatology from NIMS Hyderabad. That sequence means something to referring doctors and to informed patients. It should be legible in seconds.
  3. Take the referral. A dedicated route for physicians who suspect an immune-mediated disorder and want a specialist opinion.

Everything else — services, conditions, philosophy — supports those three.

Section 02The real problem

Why sites look AI-generated

"Looks AI-made" isn't a vague feeling. It's a specific, short list of defaults that every generator reaches for. Name them and you can avoid them.

The underlying principle

Generated design is evenly designed — every element gets the same amount of attention, so nothing has emphasis and the whole page flattens. Human design is uneven on purpose. We'll spend the visual budget on the homepage opening and Dr Deshpande's profile, and keep everything else deliberately plain.

Section 03Direction

The visual thesis: a specialist clinic, in colour

Revised — draft 02

Draft 01 proposed an austere, typographic "clinical document" look. On review you asked for something warmer and more colourful, in the register of an Indian specialist clinic site. This section and the next now describe what was actually built. The rest of the plan is unchanged.

The site is built on a four-colour clinical system: a royal blue drawn from the logo carries the brand, and teal, green and plum colour-code the four condition families. The colour is doing a job — a patient scanning for "allergy and immune concerns" follows green through the site, from the homepage card to the conditions page to the service listing.

Reference points are the specialist clinic and the well-made hospital site: a coloured hero band, contact details in a bar above the fold, credential cards, icon-led service tiles, generous rounded cards on tinted section backgrounds, and a prominent WhatsApp action. It should read as welcoming and organised rather than austere.

What it deliberately does not borrow from that genre: invented statistics, patient testimonials, stock photographs of models in lab coats, justified body text, or superlative claims. Those are the parts that make clinic sites look interchangeable — and in India, testimonials and superlatives also sit outside what a registered practitioner may advertise (Section 08).

It also stays calm. Patients arriving here are often anxious, frequently in pain, and have usually seen three doctors already. Nothing moves, pulses, pops up, or auto-plays. Hover and focus states are the only motion on the site.

Section 04Identity

Logo, palette and type

The mark

The logo is the artwork you supplied: a circular emblem in a blue-to-green gradient holding four motifs — an antibody, a knee joint, a DNA helix and leaves — above the DIRA wordmark and the line Immunity Aligned. Movement Redefined.

It is a strong asset for this practice specifically: the antibody says clinical immunology, the joint says rheumatology, and together they say what a generic cross or caduceus never could. The whole site palette is now drawn from it rather than sitting beside it.

One request for the designer

The artwork arrived as a JPEG on a white background, with bevels and a drop shadow baked into the pixels. Two consequences: the emblem sits on a white chip wherever the surface behind it isn't already white (otherwise the shadow fringes), and it will never be perfectly crisp at very small sizes. An SVG master, or a transparent PNG at 1024px or larger, would fix both. Worth asking whoever produced it — it is a five-minute export for them. Until then the site derives its assets automatically (npm run build:logo), so replacing the source file and re-running is all that is needed.

Colour

The blue and the green are sampled from the logo itself. The royal blue is the brand and fills the hero, the footer and the page bands; blue, teal, green and plum colour-code the four condition families and are used consistently sitewide, so colour encodes category rather than decorating the page. Amber is reserved strictly for actions — it is the only warm note anywhere, which makes a button unmistakable.

Typography

Unchanged from draft 01, and it holds up well against the colour: a warm serif for headings gives the pages authority that a second sans would not, and keeps the site from looking like a template.

Icons

Fifteen marks drawn for this site at a single stroke weight — consultation, second opinion, antibody, joint, pain, rehabilitation, laboratory, pharmacy, multidisciplinary care, and the contact marks. Not a thousand-icon library dropped in wholesale, which is where the interchangeable look usually comes from. The antibody icon is the logo, so the mark recurs as a motif rather than sitting in the corner unrelated to everything else.

Section 05Photography

The one thing that matters most

If you do only one thing from this document, do this one.

No amount of typography will stop a site looking generated if the images are stock. Conversely, three or four honest photographs of the actual room and the actual doctor will carry the entire site — because no generator can fake them.

What we need: a half-day with a local photographer, natural light, no flash, no lab coats posed against white backdrops.

  • One portrait of Dr Deshpande — seated, in the consulting room, looking at the camera. Not smiling broadly. Composed and attentive is the correct register for this speciality.
  • One wider shot of the consulting room, empty.
  • The waiting area and the entrance, so patients recognise the building when they arrive.
  • Physiotherapy / rehabilitation space, if it exists on site.
  • Two or three quiet detail shots — hands, a goniometer, the desk, the door plate.
If a shoot isn't possible before launch

The site is built with the photography slots already in place, each showing a visible [ADD PORTRAIT OF DR DESHPANDE] marker until a real image is dropped in. It launches perfectly well without them — colour and type carry it. What it must never launch with is stock photography of models in lab coats: patients notice, and it undoes the credibility everything else is working to build.

Booking

My recommendation for v1: WhatsApp deep link as the primary action, phone as the equal second, and a short request form as the third. Indian clinic patients overwhelmingly prefer WhatsApp, it needs no backend, and it doesn't over-promise a confirmed slot the way a calendar widget does. A real booking system can come later once you know the actual volume.

Section 07Copy

Leave the writing alone

The document you've written is unusually good. It does three things most clinic copy fails at: it admits diagnostic uncertainty, it declines to promise cures, and it uses short declarative lines with real rhythm. That voice is the strongest anti-generic asset the project has.

So the editing brief is trim, don't rewrite:

  • Cut roughly 30–40% for the web. The condition lists repeat across four sections; each appears once, in the place it belongs.
  • Keep every short line break as a line break. "Sometimes the cause is clear. / Sometimes it isn't." is doing typographic work — it should be set as displayed lines, not folded into a paragraph.
  • Keep the pull quote. "Not every symptom is an autoimmune disease. But every unexplained concern deserves a thoughtful evaluation." That's the site's whole thesis and it belongs on the homepage.
  • Fix the numbering in "Our Approach" — the source has six steps all numbered 1.
  • No superlatives get added anywhere. No "best", "leading", "top", "world-class". Partly because it's better writing, and partly because of Section 08.

Section 08Compliance

Advertising rules for Indian practitioners

Flagging this early because it constrains the copy, and it's easier to build within than retrofit.

Advertising and soliciting by registered medical practitioners in India is restricted. The operative code has been the Medical Council of India's Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics Regulations, 2002 (Chapter 6.1); the NMC's 2023 replacement regulations were notified and then withdrawn, so the position is somewhat unsettled. A factual, informational practice website is accepted practice. Promotional claims are not.

Practical rules we'll build to:

  • No patient testimonials or reviews on the site. This is the most common violation and the easiest to avoid.
  • No superlatives or comparative claims — "best rheumatologist in the city", "number one", "leading centre".
  • No before/after imagery, no success rates, no cure claims.
  • Display the NMC / State Medical Council registration number alongside qualifications. It's expected, and it also signals legitimacy to patients who know to look.
  • State fees factually or not at all — never as an offer, discount, or package.
  • If teleconsultation is offered, it falls under the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, 2020, and needs its own terms page.
Not legal advice

Worth a five-minute confirmation with Dr Deshpande's state medical council or the practice's lawyer before launch. Your existing copy already complies — no testimonials, no superlatives, no cure promises — so this is mostly about not drifting later.

Section 09Findability

How patients will actually arrive

Realistically, in this order: Google Maps, a word-of-mouth name search, a symptom search, and a referring doctor. The website is only part of that.

  1. Google Business Profile is priority one — ahead of the website, honestly. Complete profile, correct category ("Rheumatologist"), real photos, accurate hours, and the site linked. Most local patients never leave Maps.
  2. Name searches. "Dr Gaurang Deshpande rheumatologist" must return the site first. That needs the name in the title tag, a Physician and MedicalClinic schema block, and consistent name/address/phone everywhere.
  3. Symptom and condition searches are the long game. "Rheumatologist in [city]" is winnable; "positive ANA what does it mean" is where the Patient Education pages earn their keep over 6–12 months. Build the section now, fill it gradually — a few genuinely useful articles beat twenty thin ones.
  4. Performance. Most traffic will be Android on mobile data. No heavy JavaScript, self-hosted fonts, compressed images. The site should be usable on a slow connection — which the design direction gives us for free.
Name collision worth knowing about

"DIRA" is already an established term in exactly this speciality — Deficiency of the Interleukin-1 Receptor Antagonist, a rare autoinflammatory disease. Dr Deshpande almost certainly knows this. The practical consequence: the clinic will never rank for "DIRA" alone, and searches will surface the disease. Nothing needs to change about the name, but every title tag, listing and citation should read "DIRA Clinic, [City]" or "Deshpande Immunology & Rheumatology" rather than the bare acronym.

Stack

LayerChoice and reasoning
FrameworkNext.js 16, App Router, static export. Nine mostly-static pages with good SEO needs — static HTML at the edge, no server to maintain
StylingTailwind v4 with a locked custom token set, and a contrast script that verifies every colour pair. No component library — those are precisely where the interchangeable look comes from

FontsSelf-hosted, subset, preloaded. No external font request
FormsServerless handler to email, plus honeypot spam protection. Nothing patient-identifying is stored
HostingVercel or Netlify, free tier, custom domain, automatic HTTPS
AnalyticsPlausible or similar — cookieless, so no consent banner. A health site should not be running ad-network trackers

Non-negotiables

  • Keyboard navigable throughout, visible focus states, WCAG AA contrast in both light and dark. Patients with inflammatory arthritis may have real difficulty with fine pointer control — generous tap targets are a clinical courtesy, not a nicety.
  • Phone number and WhatsApp reachable from every page without scrolling on mobile.
  • No cookie banner, no chat widget, no newsletter pop-up, no exit-intent modal.

Section 12Sequence

How we get there

Five phases. Phase 1 can start the moment the first four items in Section 11 land.

01

Content edit and page architecture

Trim the source document to web length, resolve the repetition across sections, fix the numbering, and lock the nine-page structure with final headings. Nothing is designed yet.

2–3 days

02

Homepage design, built in code

No Figma stage — the homepage gets built directly as a real page so you can judge it on a phone. If the direction is wrong, this is the cheap moment to find out.

3–4 days, one round of revisions

03

Remaining eight pages

Built against the approved system. Dr Deshpande's profile and the For Doctors page get individual attention; the rest follow the established patterns.

4–5 days

04

Photography integrated, technical pass

Images placed and optimised. Schema markup, meta tags, sitemap, accessibility audit, performance testing on a throttled mobile connection, form testing.

2–3 days

05

Launch and local search

Domain, hosting, HTTPS, analytics. Google Business Profile created or claimed and fully completed, Search Console verified, sitemap submitted. Then a proofread of every page by Dr Deshpande before it goes public.

1–2 days

Roughly three weeks of working time, assuming answers come back promptly and photography doesn't hold things up. Phases 1–3 can proceed with placeholder contact details if needed, but nothing launches without them.
